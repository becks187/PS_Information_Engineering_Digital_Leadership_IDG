Measuring the Human Dimensions of Digital 
Leadership for Successful Digital Transformation
Digital leaders can use the authors’ Digital Leadership Scale to assess their own readiness and ability to accelerate 
digital transformation.
Haroon abbu, Paul Mugge, Gerhard Gudergan, Gerrit Hoeborn, and alexander Kwiatkowski
OVERVIEW: The digital transformation of organizations continues at a frenetic pace. While some companies have achieved 
trailblazer status, others are lagging because they find the transition difficult. Digital leaders play a pivotal role in digital 
transformation successes because they can inspire organizational and employee confidence in these disruptive and sometimes 
risky initiatives. We present a Digital Leadership Scale with 15 dimensions. Our scale focuses on successful leadership in 
digital transformation. We aimed to capture the qualities of leaders leading digitally mature organizations so that leaders of 
digitally developing organizations can increase their likelihood of success. We also mention the future development of a 
Digital Leadership Scale self-assessment tool that digital leaders can use to assess their own readiness and ability to accelerate 
digital initiatives.
KEYWORDS: Digital leadership, Trust, Leadership assessment, Digital transformation
Digital transformation is more about people than it is about 
digital technology. It requires organizational changes that are 
backed by leadership and driven by radical challenges to 
corporate culture. Organizations undergoing digital transfor-
mation fall into two categories: digitally developing and digitally 
mature (Gudergan et al. 2019; Mugge et al. 2020). Digitally 
mature organizations are managed differently than digitally 
developing organizations. Their leaders align human and 
financial resources with the company’s strategy; create a col-
laborative, and nimble development environment; and pro-
mote open and transparent communication (Mugge et al. 
2020). Digital transformation requires leaders to build trust 
(Mugge, Abbu, and Gudergan 2021; Gudergan et.al. 2021; 
Abbu et al. 2020). The attributes digital leaders exhibit are 
the main difference between digitally mature and digitally 
developing companies.
Digital leadership is multidimensional (Prince 2018); it 
comprises elements of authentic leadership, transactional 
leadership, and transformational leadership. In their litera-
ture review of digital leadership, Eberl and Drews (2021) 
argue that a practice-oriented and integrated scale is needed 
to measure digital leadership. Currently, no established

Study
We conducted a study to ascertain which human qualities 
influence digital leaders’ performance. We interviewed 13 
successful leaders of digitally mature organizations. We con-
sider them successful based on their track record of successful 
digital transformations in their companies, as evidenced in 
published case studies and other articles. We wanted to hear 
directly from them regarding what they are doing and what 
advice they have for other digital leaders. Our goal is to take 
the learnings from leaders of digitally mature organizations 
and help leaders of digitally developing organizations prog-
ress toward digital maturity.
Method
To find suitable interviewees, we used the maximum varia-
tion strategy of purposeful sampling (Palinkas et al. 2015). 
The variation factors were geographic representation, size of 
the company, gender, and industry representation. We devel-
oped a database of the leaders we wanted to interview. Our 
list comprised leaders associated with companies of any size, 
from a micro-startup to a conglomerate, in any industry, 
headquartered in either the United States or Germany. We 
made a deliberate attempt to include female digital leaders 
to balance the perspectives and to understand if it is harder 
for them to build trust in organizations where men hold most 
leadership positions.
Between August and October 2020, we conducted 13 
semi-structured interviews (3 women, 10 men). At least two 
members of the research team participated in each interview. 
We conducted interviews face-to-face and via Zoom. We 
asked interviewees five questions that we felt would identify 
what actions leaders are taking and how and why they pur-
sue them:
1. What is your key theme 
(main message) for trans-
forming the organization?
2. What is your organization’s 
digital strategy and vision?
3. What actions have you taken 
to achieve your theme?
4. What results have you wit-
nessed to date?
5. What advice do you have for 
other digital leaders?
We audio-recorded the interview 
with the interviewees’ consent 
and transcribed them for data 
analysis. Participation was vol-
untary and without compensa-
tion. The interviewees hold 
various senior management 
positions: seven CEOs, two business unit heads (BUHs) 
developing digital products, two functional heads (FHs) lead-
ing corporate functions, and two chief digital officers (CDOs) 
(Table 1). Together, the digital leaders we interviewed rep-
resented companies with combined revenues of more than 
$225B and nearly 1.2 million employees.
We used an inductive approach to reduce the extensive 
text data gained from the interviews (Thomas 2006). We 
reviewed the interview transcripts several times to identify 
and code the categories. To identify leadership traits specific 
to digital transformation, we used conventional content anal-
ysis, which is exploratory and allows the categories and 
names for categories to flow from the data (Hsieh and 
Shannon 2005).
We based our interpretations solely on what the interview-
ees said. We reviewed the transcripts for direct quotations, 
analyzed relevant text, and reviewed the final interview report 
with the interviewees to confirm each quotation’s accuracy. 
Using content analysis—both automated and manual—we 
conceptionally analyzed the presence of certain words, themes, 
or concepts within the text of our interviews and then sum-
marized them into manageable code categories. We first coded 
the text automatically using software to identify possible cat-
egories. We then took the terms that fit the dimensions and 
manually searched the interview text to see if the interviewee 
mentioned them or implied them. We counted the number 
of participants mentioning each dimension and created a table 
of our content analysis results (Table 2).

Digital Leadership Scale
Based on our results, we developed a Digital Leadership Scale 
consisting of 15 human dimensions. For each dimension, we 
provide a definition based on relevant literature, discuss prior 
research pertaining to its relevance to digital leadership, and 
list existing scales, if any. We provide a practical definition 
to ensure it is applicable for digital leadership. We then list 
three questions (“items”) digital leaders can use to measure 
each dimension. We considered items from existing scales 
and constructs only conceptually while creating new items 
for each of the 15 dimensions, since it is not good practice 
to detach items from their original scale context (Kraus and 
Kreitenweis 2020). Consistent with our prior research, we 
have categorized these 15 dimensions according to Stephen 
Covey’s (2006) 4 Cores of Credibility: Integrity, Intent, 
Capabilities, and Results (Figure 1).
#1: Honesty
Rotter (1967, p. 651) defines honesty as “the expectancy 
that the word, promise, verbal or written statement of 
another individual or group can be relied upon.” Servant 
leadership, for example, values honesty highly (Russell and 
Stone 2002). A major study by IBM showed that role mod-
eling by executives is the most important factor for success-
ful change initiatives (Jorgensen, Bruehl, and Franke 2014), 
and role models must be credible and embody what they 
say. Honesty is the most important factor to generate cred-
ibility in a leader (Kouzes and Posner 2011). The measure-
ment of honesty is part of the HEXAGO Personality Scale 
(Ashton and Lee 2009) and Satow’s B5T scale (Satow 2012). 
For measuring honesty in the context of digital transfor -
mation, we are concerned with how it manifests interper -
sonally, especially how the digital leader conveys honesty 
and how it contributes to digital transformation success.
Working Definition: An assessment of the leader’s moral 
character that connotes positive and virtuous attributes such 
as integrity, truthfulness, and straightforwardness. Honesty 
also involves being trustworthy, loyal, fair, and sincere.
As a leader of digital initiatives, I …
1. Address concerns employees have with the digital strategy 
publicly.
2. Give employees clear answers, even to critical 
questions.

#2: Humility
From an interpersonal perspective, humility “involves reg -
ulation of egotism and cultivation of other-orientation 
rather than a selfish preoccupation with one’s own needs” 
(Davis et al. 2017, p. 4). Leaders manage to build trust 
through selfless behavior. According to the servant lead-
ership theory, a good leader’s most important task is to 
serve other people (Baggett 1997). Humility is a necessary 
dimension to understanding employees’ true needs first. 
This dimension does not negate leaders representing their 
own interests; however, humility implies putting aside 
one’s own opinion and listening empathetically to employ-
ees to gain new insights, even if differences of opinion 
exist. Neilsen and Marrone (2018) have studied the com-
ponents included in the humility concept. Humility is also 
part of the HEXAGO Personality Scale (Ashton and Lee 
2009). In the context of digital transformation, we are 
focused on how the leader displays humility externally 
within a business interaction.
Working Definition: An assessment of the leader’s  
modest view of their own importance—that is, their 
humbleness.
As a leader of digital initiatives, I …
1. Respect the opinions of our employees, even if it is not 
the same as mine.
2. Value every contribution of every individual, no matter 
how big or small it may be.
3. Gratefully accept any feedback both positive and 
negative.

#4: Ethical AI
Recent advances in machine learning and neural networks 
have enabled artificial intelligence (AI) to be used for hiring, 
criminal justice, health care, and other applications. The 
prevalence of AI in many industries has brought AI ethics to 
the forefront of digital leadership. As promising as machine 
learning technology is, DeBrusk (2018) points out how it 
can also be susceptible to unintended biases that require 
careful planning to avoid. Ethical leadership represents 
shared facets with authentic, spiritual, and transformational 
leadership such as altruism, integrity, and role modeling 
(Brown and Treviño 2006). Existing scales that measure eth-
ics, such as the Ethical Leadership Scale (Brown, Treviño, 
and Harrison 2005) and the Leadership Virtues Questionnaire 
(Riggio et al. 2010), lack an ethical technology assessment. 
Ethical concerns related to the application of AI in data usage 
and managerial activities is an important topic for digital 
leaders to address (Cortellazzo, Bruni, and Zampieri 2019).
Working Definition: An assessment of a leader’s ability 
to do everything in their power to ensure the results of AI 
and other technologies employing machine learning models, 
are fair and explainable.
As a leader of digital initiatives, I …
1. Make sure senior management understands the need for 
building trustworthy AI that ensures fairness and accu-
racy, which meets their privacy and compliance 
requirements.
2. Put processes in place to ensure no human biases creep 
into algorithms and affect outcomes.
3. Develop and encourage participation in training sessions 
related to ethics in AI.
#5: Growth Mindset
A growth mindset explores the extent to which people believe 
they can become smarter if they work at it. It is based on the 
belief that “your basic qualities are things you can cultivate 
through your efforts” (Dweck 2016, p. 7). Research shows 
that individuals with a growth mindset develop important 
skills for managing change in a professional context, such as 
more effective problem-solving strategies and dealing with 
feedback (Gottfredson and Reina 2020). In the area of digital 
transformation, leaders with a growth mindset look for new 
opportunities to realize their visions (Carpenter 2012), which 
requires that they focus on creative, innovative, and some-
times risky tactics (Renko et al. 2012). A growth mindset is 
also important to motivate one’s employees. For example, 
Petrucci and Rivera (2018) and Prince (2018) emphasize the 
relevance of a growth mindset for digital leaders in that it 
encourages employees to be curious, think alternatively, and 
expand their knowledge in accordance with “lifelong learn-
ing.” Dweck’s (1999, 2016) Growth Mindset Scale consists of 
items that focus on the belief that intelligence has a fluid com-
ponent, that it can grow. We have adopted this basic idea and 
changed it contextually for a business environment.
Working Definition: An assessment of the leader’s desire 
to tackle new challenges and grow as a person.
As a leader of digital initiatives, I …
1. Don’t view failure as a disappointment but as a learning 
experience that can lead to change.
2. Believe that all people, including myself, can develop their 
capabilities to do new things, regardless of where they 
come from.
3. Accept new challenges, take advantage of feedback, and 
provide timely feedback to subordinates.
#6: Transparent Agenda
Transparency includes openness, freedom of information, 
clarity, accuracy, and the timely release of relevant informa-
tion (Bernstein 2017). It enables employee participation and 
informs them of the opportunities and risks of digital trans-
formation so that they can be rational in their judgments. 
Transparency can lead to targeted involvement of employ-
ees—a decisive success factor for digital transformation—sig -
nificantly reducing resistance to change (Wagner 2014). 
Transparency is one way to check whether a leader really 
stands behind what they are promoting (“walking the talk”).

#7: Data Focus
The focus on data enables improvements in decision-making 
processes (Weiner, Balijepally, and Tanniru 2015), which is 
necessary for gaining an edge over competitors and for plan-
ning digital transformation measures (Ashwell 2017). The 
ability to process, analyze, prioritize, and subsequently make 
decisions based on data has become an essential skill for 
success. No seminal definition or scale exists for data focus, 
but it focuses on the leadership behaviors associated with 
business analytics and business intelligence that are neces-
sary to transform data into business insights (Pratt and 
Fruhlinger 2019). Data focus is a key dimension for digital 
leaders because while most companies are engaged in trying 
to scale analytics, only a few are truly succeeding at it (Bisson 
et al. 2018).
Working Definition: An assessment of a leader’s ability 
to encourage the application of data and analytics every-
where, including their own decision-making.
As a leader of digital initiatives, I …
1. Use data analytics to drive our major operational and stra-
tegic decisions.
2. Trust data more than my intuition.
3. Make sure the right people in their organization have the 
training and skills necessary to work with data.
#8: Inspire Engagement
Inspiring engagement is a central element of transformational 
leadership and contributes significantly to digital transformation 
success. Motivation and inspiration have always been essential 
skills that any successful leader must master (Horner-Long and 
Schoenberg 2002). A central aspect of this notion of inspiring 
engagement is the leader’s ability to make employees feel they 
are a significant part of the company transformation and to 
avoid active and passive forms of resistance as much as possible. 
It is also about the ability to create a vision in a participatory 
manner—that is, staff participate in creating the vision—because 
transformative visions are crucial to digital transformation suc-
cess. Executives must establish the connection between that 
company vision and the employees (Kane et  al. 2019). 
Researchers have pointed out the lack of a generally accepted 
conceptualization of employee engagement or a tool to measure 
it (Witemeyer, Ellen, and Straub 2013). In this study, employee 
engagement is an attitude towards one’s work in one’s organi-
zation, comprising feelings of vigor, dedication, and absorption; 
cognitive appraisals of psychological empowerment; and moti-
vation to act in the service of the organization’s goals.
Working Definition: An assessment of a leader’s ability 
to stimulate people’s interest in or enthusiasm for doing 
something.
As a leader of digital initiatives, I …
1. Let people know that their input matters and that they 
have the autonomy to develop and implement new ideas.
2. Create a sense of urgency, enthusiasm, and pride at work.
3. Appreciate our willingness to expend the effort necessary 
to persist in the face of challenges.
#9: Storytelling
Values and principles cannot make an impact on employees 
unless they are woven into emotional reference stories (Merath 
and Nau 2017). Good stories arouse emotions and visual images 
and thus have an entertaining and informative effect (Morgan 
and Dennehy 1997). In this way, the audience—whether a 
group or an individual—is addressed holistically both cogni-
tively and emotionally (Lippmann, Pfister and Jörg 2019). 
Stories are an efficient way to disseminate information and a 
transformative vision; they help link the past, present, and 
future, create a sense of continuity among employees and make 
change—especially radical change—seem doable (Boal and 
Schultz 2007). A good story can be equally effective in informal 
word-of-mouth communication between employees. Therefore, 
organizations must empower their innovation teams to practice 
the art and science of innovation storytelling (Taylor 2021). 
Measuring storytelling primarily aims for the impact of story-
telling (Rossetti and Wall 2017). In our study, we are primarily 
focused on leaders’ awareness of the need for effective story-
telling and its use in a targeted manner.

inspire employees and gain buy-in.

#10: Digital Literacy
A deeper understanding of the technological possibilities 
enables leaders to create an adequate vision and inspire 
employees to get involved in the first place (Kane et al. 2019). 
If these competencies are lacking, digital leaders must develop 
such skills and foster them within the team to exploit the 
benefits of digital transformation efficiently (Cortellazzo, 
Bruni, and Zampieri 2019). Demonstrating relevant expertise 
is essential to increase a leader’s credibility (Yukl 1998). 
Although there are scales to measure certain components of 
digital literacy (Jin et al. 2020; Carretero, Vuorikari, and Punie 
2017), they fail to encompass the broad nature of digital lit-
eracy (Chetty et al. 2018). Since digital literacy priorities vary 
from industry to industry, our questions are primarily con-
cerned with the extent to which digital leaders demand and 
promote digital literacy in themselves and their employees.
Working Definition: An assessment of a leader’s ability 
to develop digital competencies within the leadership team.
As a leader of digital initiatives, I …
1. Make sure that everyone on the direct report team has 
the necessary technical skills to implement our digital 
strategy.
2. Regularly seek outside resources (advice of thought 
leaders, joint research projects, partnerships, executive 
education, etc.) to maintain the organization’s digital 
talents.
3. Ensure the entire leadership team knows the strategic and 
operational benefits of our digital technologies.
#11: Positive Attitude
A positive attitude is necessary to express emotionalizing 
reactions such as recognition, gratitude, and praise. Leaders 
with positive attitudes are more resilient, socially inte-
grated, and high-performing, which leads to increased help-
fulness, flexibility, empathy, and creativity (Creusen, 
Eschemann, and Johann 2010). Through positive attitudes 
and a positive leadership style, leaders can elevate their 
employees’ confidence, commitment, and well-being long 
term (Liu, Siu, and Shi 2010). The psychometric scales in 
this space are more character-orientated—how this positiv-
ity manifests in the business context is more important for 
the digital leader.
Working Definition: An assessment of a leader’s ability 
to carry themselves like champions and purposefully show 
their positivity in how they behave and communicate.
As a leader of digital initiatives, I …
1. Advocate consistently for the best interests of our 
company.
2. Take it on myself to be accountable.
3. Am committed to the success of our company.
#12: Skills Acquisition
UNESCO (2018) defines digital skills as “a range of abilities 
to use digital devices, communication applications, and net-
works to access and manage information. They enable people 
to create and share digital content, communicate and collab-
orate, and solve problems for effective and creative self-ful-
fillment in life, learning, work, and social activities at large.” 
Identifying, attracting, and motivating people with these 
ever-evolving skill sets is of paramount importance to digital 
leaders. To thrive in the connected economy and society, 
digital skills must also function together with other abilities 
such as strong literacy and numeracy skills, critical and inno-
vative thinking, complex problem solving, ability to collab-
orate, and socio-emotional skills. Soft skills such as agility, 
creativity, teamwork, and ability to learn are the skills that 
will survive the automated future (Maurer 2019).
Working Definition: An assessment of a leader’s ability 
to influence digital talent acquisition and development for 
the organization.
As a leader of digital initiatives, I …
1. Am personally involved in our efforts to recruit digital 
talents to the organization.
2. Influence efforts to retrain and rebalance the organiza-
tion’s digital skills.
3. Have initiated active programs with local colleges and 
universities to recruit digitally talented people.
#13: Knowledge Sharing
An international study of 1,700 CEOs found that outper -
forming organizations have leaders who create a culture of 
openness, connectivity, and the highest level of collaboration 
in the digital world (Berman and Korsten 2014). Developing 
a system that enables knowledge dissemination from an indi-
vidual to the group is the most effective way to spread knowl-
edge and expertise throughout an organization (Boe and 
Torgersen 2018). Perception- and activity-based methods are 
the primary approaches to measure knowledge sharing in 
organizations (Long, Siau, and Howell 2007). Our assessment 
focuses on the role of the leader, who must actively promote 
knowledge sharing for successful digital transformation.
Working Definition: An assessment of a leader’s ability 
to enable access to knowledge and improve learning.
As a leader of digital initiatives, I …
1. Insist we share our knowledge, particularly with other 
departments/organizations struggling with digital 
transformation.
2. Give every employee the opportunity to present “what 
they are learning/have learned” to the rest of the 
organization.

3. Have implemented “safety nets” that encourage experi-

#14: Participative Style
Digital leaders are expected to strive for a leadership style 
in which they not only allow their team to participate but 
also participate in team activities themselves (Schwarzmüller 
et  al. 2018). By doing so, they gain and maintain trust 
within their team (Carte, Chidambaram, and Becker 2006). 
Pentland (2018) showed that team-oriented leaders 
actively engage people in short, high-energy conversations; 
they are democratic with their employees’ time; they com-
municate with everyone equally, and they ensure that all 
team members get a chance to contribute. They engage in 
“stimulated but focused listening” and listen as much or 
more than they talk, and they are usually engaged with 
whomever they are listening to. Teaming leadership is also 
about creating open spaces, giving team members freedom 
to choose their efforts, and motivating them to voluntarily 
make a positive effort to complete tasks (Rahmani, Roels, 
and Karmarkar 2018). Servant leadership emphasizes 
“increased service to others; a holistic approach to work; 
promoting a sense of community; and the sharing of power 
in decision making” (Spears, Greenleaf, and Covey 2002, 
p. 4). Several measurement scales—for example, the one 
proposed Bhatti et al. (2019)—have examined the effects 
of participative, team-oriented leadership and organiza-
tional citizenship behavior rela tion ships.
Working Definition: An assessment of a leader’s ability 
to develop a collaborative and inclusive way to approach 
problems and opportunities.
As a leader of digital initiatives, I …
1. Require that we celebrate “small wins,” as well as major 
accomplishments, to motivate employees.
2. Prefer “rolling up my sleeves” and working directly with 
the development teams.
3. Make time for people who have questions.
#15: Track Record
This dimension reflects a transactional facet of digital lead-
ership, but regarding the leader themselves. This critical 
reflection enables a down-to-earth self-assessment—perhaps 
the leader needs to further build competencies, deal with 
new disruption, or improve digital literacy. For track record, 
successful transformation is also always about measurable 
results, whether positive or negative (Prince 2018). Several 
business results metrics could be included here—for example, 
improved outcomes and increased customer experience. 
Rather than listing them out, we chose to keep the measure-
ment at a higher level—namely, did the digital initiative pro-
duce meaningful results?
Working Definition: An assessment of the leader’s dig-
ital initiatives to produce meaningful results.
As a leader of digital initiatives, I …
1. Changed an aspect of business using digital methods.
2. Reduced the cycle time or operational cost of the business 
significantly.
3. Created an overall better customer experience.
Managerial Implications
We hypothesized and found support for the fact that the 
human dimensions of digital leaders are the foundation  
of digital transformation success. We recommend leaders 
focus on these 15 key dimensions we outline in our Digital 
Leadership Scale. We highlight three key takeaways:
1. Building trust and credibility are at the core of effective 
digital leadership. Credible leaders walk the talk, and 
employees learn by observing their leaders.
2. Employees should be at the center of your digital strategy. 
Align employees with business goals and clearly commu-
nicate your plan and vision for the company to them. Listen 
to your employees, understand what they are thinking, 
and integrate employee feedback into your communica-
tions and operations strategy. Doing so will help you build 
an innovative, transparent, and inclusive culture.
3. Digital leaders must make conscious efforts to ensure 
trustworthiness in the use of digital technologies such as 
AI and machine learning. They must ensure these tech-
nologies are used in a way that’s fair, respectful of indi-
vidual privacy, and beneficial to society.
Future research
We identified 15 human dimensions of digital leadership that 
we consider key to digital transformation success. Given the 
urgency and importance of digital transformation and the 
critical role digital leaders play in successful execution, we 
are currently working on two initiatives.

these 15 dimensions on a five-point Likert scale. We will 
administer this scale to digital leaders. Using statistical meth-

develop a statistically valid and reliable scale that measures 
key dimensions of digital leadership.

Limitations
The dimensions presented should be checked for their dis-
tinctiveness. Within the research discussions, for example, 
it emerged that the Storytelling dimension can be a sub-item 
of the Inspire Engagement dimension. Measuring character 
traits is admittedly difficult, especially since the measure-
ments are mostly self-reported questionnaires. As a reflexive 
assessment, such a measurement fulfills the criterion of 
practical relevance and can be classified as useful. 
Nevertheless, we recommend a supplementary review and 
discussion of the dimensions to increase objectivity and use-
fulness. Additionally, the inductive reasoning approach used 
is limited as it begins with our observation of the role of 
digital leadership in digital transformation success. Our 
observations are based on a set of interviews, yet we may 
have inadvertently overlooked important factors. Future 
research should focus on quantitative studies to investigate 
the aspects found in this study—namely, its intercorrelations 
and their relevance depending on industry and company 
demographics.
Conclusion
We presented a prescriptive assessment for leaders in digital 
transformation by identifying the human dimensions required 
to successfully lead a digital transformation initiative. In line 
with the motto “reflection enables insight for improvement,” 
we intend for the Digital Leadership Scale to inspire leaders 
to improve their leadership style based on science. Leaders 
can use the 15 dimensions we identified for critical self-reflec-
tion. The overarching insight derived from this study is that 
the human dimensions of leadership are critical to a successful 
digital transformation.
