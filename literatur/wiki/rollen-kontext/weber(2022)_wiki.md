# The Digital Transformation Leadership Framework: Conceptual and Empirical Insights into Leadership Roles in Technology-Driven Business Environments

> Authors: Ellen Weber, Eva-Helen Krehl, and Marion Büttgen, Institute of Marketing & Management, University of Hohenheim, Stuttgart, Germany. Published in *Journal of Leadership Studies*, Volume 16, Number 1, 2022. DOI:10.1002/jls.21810 [Page 1].

---

## Overview

The article addresses leadership in the context of digital transformation. "The powerful and disruptive changes associated with digital transformation make leadership more complex than before, which results in new and demanding challenges for companies and leaders alike." [Page 1]

The research builds on the competing values framework (CVF), "which postulates that leaders must adopt multiple roles and behaviors," and aims "to identify leadership roles appropriate in digital transformation and subsequently investigate whether the existing CVF roles are still relevant." [Page 1–2]

Stated methods and outcomes: "A qualitative study was conducted to identify appropriate leadership behaviors in digital transformation. Additionally, a measurement scale for the identified leadership roles was developed and tested. Furthermore, multidimensional scaling (MDS) was used to test the degree-of-fit between the identified leadership roles in digital transformation and the four CVF quadrants." [Page 2]

Stated overall finding: "The results reveal that new leadership roles emerge in the context of digital transformation. Some original CVF roles are still relevant, but others should be modified. Overall, the findings indicate that leaders need a broad behavioral complexity to master the emerging leadership challenges in digital transformation." [Page 2]

The two stated purposes: "one purpose of the current study was to identify leadership roles that appropriately address the leadership challenges digital transformation brings. The second purpose was to collate the identified roles with the existing leadership roles of the CVF and thereby evaluate their appropriateness in times of digital transformation." [Page 2]

## Core Concepts / Findings

### 1. Digital Transformation (context)

- Defined in the text as "a disruptive change process caused by new digital technologies that influence all aspects of life (Verhoef et al., 2021; Vial, 2019)." [Page 2]
- "Digital transformation has dramatically changed the nature of work (Petrucci & Rivera, 2018)." [Page 2]
- "Many employees work in at least partly virtual, self-organized, nonhierarchical teams, using agile methods and digital technologies to generate innovative products and services (Kane et al., 2019)." [Page 2]
- It "increase[s] leadership complexity while simultaneously require[s] considerable flexibility (Ford et al., 2021)." [Page 2]

### 2. The Competing Values Framework (CVF)

- The CVF "(Quinn, 1988; Quinn & Rohrbaugh, 1981) highlights the trade-offs, paradoxes, and contradictions within organizations and their leaders (Belasen & Frank, 2008)." [Page 3]
- It "overcomes viewing leadership behaviors in an either/or fashion by emphasizing that leadership effectiveness requires the integration of competing leadership roles (Lawrence et al., 2009)." [Page 3]
- "The CVF defines two dimensions that express the competing values leaders face in complex organizational environments: a flexible versus stable structure and an internal versus external focus (Kalliath et al., 1999)." [Page 3]
- "Initially, Quinn (1988) identified eight leadership roles (i.e., facilitator, mentor, innovator, broker, monitor, coordinator, producer, and director). These roles are embedded in four quadrants, named Collaborate, Create, Control, and Compete (see Figure 1)." [Page 3]
- "The capacity to exhibit such a broad array of behavior is called behavioral complexity and is related to greater managerial and leadership effectiveness (Denison et al., 1995; Vilkinas et al., 2020)." [Page 3]
- Researchers "called to retest the factor structure of the CVF roles, as it depends mainly on the context in which they are embedded and evaluated (Belasen & Frank, 2008)." [Page 3]

### 3. Study 1 — Qualitative Study (Identification of Roles)

- "A qualitative approach was applied to systematically study which specific leadership behaviors are appropriate to manage the challenges associated with digital transformation. Thirty in-depth interviews with managers and employees were conducted." [Page 3–4]
- Sample: "The interviewee included 4 females and 26 males, of whom most were between 31 and 40 years old (33.3%)." [Page 4]
- "Data saturation was reached at approximately 30 interviews, after which no new information appeared in the data (Guest et al., 2006)." [Page 4]
- "The interview sessions lasted approximately 30 minutes each." [Page 4]
- "The data were reviewed with a thematic analysis ... specifically following the thematic analysis steps outlined by Braun and Clarke (2006)." [Page 4]
- "To ensure the trustworthiness of the findings, both researcher and data triangulation was applied (Bluhm et al., 2011). All reliability thresholds were met." [Page 4]
- Result: "Based on the responses, six different leadership roles were identified, each characterized by specific leadership behaviors: the digital pioneer, the innovator, the enabler, the mentor, the networker, and the manager." [Page 4]

### 4. The Six Initially Identified Leadership Roles

| Role | Verbatim description |
|------|----------------------|
| Digital Pioneer | "A digital pioneer is a leader who understands the drivers of digital transformation and identifies both business opportunities and risks arising from digital transformation. Such leaders are able to detect early digital trends ... they conceptualize a clear digital vision and strategy." [Page 4] |
| Innovator | "the innovator is a leader who drives and implements change processes based on innovative and creative ideas. These leaders show enthusiasm and inspire their employees by inventing the new." [Page 4] |
| Enabler | "These leaders create agile, flexible, and empowering work settings for their team members ... enable a culture of learning by supporting trial and error. Further, they enable working in collaborative, nonhierarchical, interdisciplinary, and intercultural teams." [Page 4] |
| Mentor | "the leader acts as a relationship manager who strives to establish strong and trusting relationships with employees, while providing individual support, mentoring, and feedback." [Page 4] |
| Networker | "a leader as a networker develops, scans, and maintains interdisciplinary networks within and outside the organization." [Page 4] |
| Manager | "a leader as a manager 'does things right.' The manager is strongly oriented to task and goal achievement ... attends closely to relevant key performance indicators." [Page 4–5] |

### 5. Conceptualizing the Digital Transformation Leadership Framework

- Inter-relationships: "The digital pioneer and the innovator have in common that they focus on digital transformation ... However, the digital pioneer is strategically oriented, while the innovator concentrates on the operative level." [Page 5]
- "Comparing the enabler and mentor roles reveals that they both comprise relation-oriented leadership behaviors ... an enabler focuses on the whole team ... whereas the mentor concentrates on each employee." [Page 5]
- "The roles of the networker and the manager show the least overlap with the other leadership roles ... Both roles can be interpreted as relatively traditional although they remain relevant in the digital transformation context." [Page 5]

Relationships with CVF roles:
- "The manager role overlaps with the four CVF roles monitor, producer, director, and coordinator." [Page 5]
- "the enabler, mentor, networker, and innovator roles build on the CVF roles facilitator, mentor, broker, and innovator; concluding that these roles remain important in the digital age." [Page 5]
- "the digital pioneer role is an entirely new component, which is not described or articulated in the original CVF." [Page 5]

Framework assumptions:
- "the digital transformation leadership framework should not be interpreted as an entirely new leadership approach. Instead, the digital transformation leadership framework is a full-range leadership approach with some overlap regarding existing leadership concepts." [Page 5]
- "a leader might not exhibit all digital transformation leadership roles simultaneously. It is more likely that a leader will exhibit the digital transformation leadership roles in varying degrees, depending on the particular leadership situation." [Page 5–6]

### 6. Scale Development Process (Studies 2–4)

The scale development "contained three phases: item development, test of content validity, and empirical evaluation (see Table 1)." [Page 6]

- **Study 2 — Item Development:** Items "generated based on (a) their conceptualizations, (b) relevant literature concerning various leadership measurements ... and (c) the results of 30 in-depth interviews with digital transformation experts ... resulting in an initial set of 42 items." [Page 6]
- **Study 3 — Test of Content Validity:** "a classifying study was conducted (Liden & Maslyn, 1998), and aligned with Schriesheim et al.'s (1993) Q-factor analysis technique." Involved "42 external experts ... 33.3% were female, with an average age of 37.5 years (SD = 10.16)." [Page 6]
  - A seventh role emerged: "the digital mentee role was created. Leaders as digital mentees seek advice from their employees on digital issues and refine their digital skills through the employees' input." [Page 6]
  - "Only items that achieved a minimum of 80% accuracy in the digital transformation experts' classification were included." [Page 6]
  - "The final item instrument consists of 39 items measuring leaders' self-perceptions ... using Likert scales ranging from 1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree." [Page 6]
- **Study 4 — Empirical Evaluation:** "Data from two independent samples of German employees were collected." [Page 6–7] Sample A = 263 employees; Sample B = 294 employees [Page 7–8].
  - EFA: "the rotated factor structure revealed seven distinguished factors that explained a significant amount of the variance (67.70%)." [Page 8]
  - CFA: "The CFA results gave a satisfactory fit (χ2 = 1,122.941; df = 664; p < .001; RMSEA = .051; SRMR = .058; CFI = .945). All 39 items showed significant standardized factor loadings (range .63–.92, p < .01)." [Page 8]
  - "all seven constructs met Fornell and Larcker's (1981) criterion for discriminant validity." [Page 8]

### 7. Predictive Validity (Outcome Variables)

Three outcome variables: "innovative job performance, knowledge sharing, and digital maturity." [Page 8]

- "employees who perceived their leaders as enablers (b = .13, p < .1) and innovators (b = .19, p < .05) indicated higher levels of innovative job performance." [Page 8]
- "Employees who perceived their leaders as networkers (b = .17, p < .05), enablers (b = .18, p < .05), and digital mentees (b = .20, p < .01) reported higher levels of knowledge sharing." [Page 8]
- "Leaders who were perceived as innovators (b = −.34, p < .01) had a negative impact on employees' knowledge sharing." [Page 8]
- "organizations' digital maturity was significantly affected by leaders who were perceived as digital pioneers (b = .35, p < .01) and managers (b = .15, p < .05)." [Page 8]

### 8. Study 5 — Spatial Arrangement (MDS)

Note: the text gives two different sample sizes for the MDS leaders sample.
- Table 1 states: "Sample size: n = 196 leaders" [Page 7].
- The body text states: "another study with 165 leaders (Study 5) was conducted." [Page 9]
- "In terms of the hierarchical level, the majority of the leaders (67.88%) headed a business unit." [Page 9]
- "the MDS was conducted using SPSS PROXSCAL. The results of the MDS indicated a two-dimensional solution." [Page 9]

Dimension 1 (horizontal): "supports the interpretation of an internal-external focus (Hooijberg & Choi, 2000)."
- Internally focused: "the enabler, the mentor, the digital mentee, and the manager." [Page 9]
- Externally focused: "the digital pioneer, the innovator, and the networker." [Page 9]

Dimension 2 (vertical): "does not support Quinn's (1988) stable-flexible interpretation ... Instead, the results indicated that the vertical axis is better represented by a task-relation orientation, which is in line with the reformulation of Vilkinas and Cartan (2006)." [Page 9]
- Task-oriented quadrants: "the digital pioneer, the innovator, the manager, and the digital mentee." [Page 9]
- Relation-focused quadrants: "the mentor, the enabler, and the networker." [Page 9]

### 9. Discussion / Key Conclusions

- "the innovator could have a dark side, pointing to a 'double-edged sword pattern' (Diebig et al., 2016) ... employees' perceptions of their leaders as innovators enhance their innovative job performance while simultaneously decrease their knowledge sharing activities." [Page 10]
- "new leadership roles emerge in the context of digital transformation (i.e., the digital pioneer and digital mentee roles). Some original CVF roles, such as the innovator or the mentor, are still relevant, but others have become less important (e.g., the manager)." [Page 10–11]
- "the original stable-flexible dimension (Quinn, 1988) is no longer supportable in a dynamic business environment ... the dimension was redefined with a task-relation orientation." [Page 11]

### 10. Practical Implications

Organizational actions should help leaders develop "(a) the ability to exhibit all seven digital transformation leadership roles, (b) an understanding of the roles to deploy them properly, and (c) the ability to switch between the different roles." [Page 11]

### 11. Limitations and Further Research

- "it would be beneficial to gain knowledge on the influence of situational contingencies on the digital transformation leadership roles." [Page 11]
- "Future research should pay more attention to potential endogeneity bias (Shockley et al., 2016)." [Page 11]
- "the conducted scale development process is based on cross-sectional study designs, which cannot reveal any changes in leadership behavior over time. Further studies might therefore use longitudinal data." [Page 11–12]

### 12. The Digital Mentee Role (Seventh Role)

Operationalized via six items; representative items include "DM_2 As a leader, I learn from my employees how to use digital tools that I do not know yet." (EFA .90, CFA .84) and "DM_3 As a leader, I seek advice from my employees on digital issues." (EFA .99, CFA .81) [Page 16]
