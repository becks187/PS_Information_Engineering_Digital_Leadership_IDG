# Wiki: Hansen et al. (2025) — "Technology isn't enough for Industry 4.0: on SMEs and hindrances to digital transformation"

## 2. Overview

The paper investigates why digital transformation is slow in small- and medium-sized manufacturing enterprises (SMEs), arguing that the challenge is not purely technological. "In light of this, we argue that the digitalisation challenge is not purely technological." [Page 3]

- **Research question:** "How can the approach to competence development in SMEs explain patterns of slow digital transformation?" [Page 2]
- **Empirical base:** "we investigate 30 manufacturing SMEs pursuing digitalisation." [Page 2]; all cases "were from the Danish manufacturing sector" [Page 6].
- **Theoretical departure point:** Ghobakhloo's (2020) model of determinants for smart manufacturing adoption. "We take our point of departure in the model of determinants by Ghobakhloo (2020)" [Page 6].
- **Core argument:** "we argue how a scaffold is needed to support SMEs in the early stages of digitalisation to increase understanding of core concepts, i.e. Industry 4.0 concepts and available technologies, data handling, integration capability, upskilling and training, and strategic management capabilities." [Page 2]
- **Proposal:** "We propose that a scaffolding approach to competence development can enable knowledge acquisition and the formation of digital transformation strategies." [Page 2]

## 3. Core Concepts / Findings

### 3.1. Industry 4.0 and Industry 5.0

- Industry 4.0 origin: "Since the introduction of industry 4.0 as a German strategic initiative in 2011 (Kagermann et al. 2013)" [Page 2].
- Smart manufacturing: the authors note "we use the term industry 4.0 and smart manufacturing interchangeably throughout the article." [Page 18]
- Industry 5.0: "Industry 5.0 generally relies on the same technological foundation as industry 4.0, although it focuses more on a human-centric approach (Ibid.). ... It is more value-driven than technology-driven like industry 4.0 (Xu et al. 2021)." [Page 3]
- Relationship between the two: "if the digital transformation of companies pursuing industry 4.0 is slow and stuck in early stages, then industry 5.0 must be presumed basically non-existent." [Page 3]
- Critique of Industry 4.0: "it has also been criticised for being too focused on technology and less so on the role of the industry workers and how to build up the needed competencies (Lassen and Waehrens 2021; Neumann et al. 2021)." [Page 3]

### 3.2. SME Context

- Prevalence: SMEs "make up a large proportion of the manufacturing industry (99.8% in Europe Gorgels et al. 2022) but are very slow to adopt smart technology (Buer et al. 2020; Lassen and Waehrens 2021)." [Page 3]
- SME advantages: "SMEs do hold some advantages over large and multi-national enterprises when it comes to digitalisation, e.g. lower organisational complexity and fewer barriers to collaboration (Horváth and Szabó 2019)." [Page 5]
- SME disadvantages: "large-scale digital transformation initiatives are financially riskier for SMEs, and they often lack IT skills, which leads to a need for external support (Hansen and Hansen 2023; Moeuf et al. 2020)." [Page 5]
- Common barriers in literature: "resource availability ..., lack of knowledge ..., lack of skills ..., and lack of technological and strategical expertise (Moeuf et al. 2020)" [Page 2].

### 3.3. Competencies for Digital Transformation (Hecklau et al. 2016)

"according to Hecklau et al. (2016), there is generally a need for technical, methodological, social, and personal competencies" [Page 3]:

- **Technical competencies:** "Technical competencies are needed to identify and build the needed connectivity. ... Specialised technical knowledge, coding skills and cyber security awareness are necessary to navigate and create this technical environment." [Page 3]
- **Methodological competencies:** "needed due to the flat hierarchical structure of smart manufacturing enterprises and increased communication across devices and people. ... increases the need for process knowledge and the ability to seek out and solve errors through data analysis and interpretation skills." [Page 3]
- **Social competencies:** "needed to effectively use the increased communication ability across departments through accessible IT platforms (e.g. Slack or Teams). ... Good communication skills are essential in order to share knowledge among peers" [Page 3].
- **Personal competencies:** "pertains to people's attitude towards change and efforts in learning new knowledge. ... It is important to be able to accept this change in how work tasks are more flexibly structured (Hecklau et al. 2016)." [Pages 3–4]
- Caution: "almost purely fixating on technical competences may create a bottle-neck for smart manufacturing adoption as foundational competence areas are overlooked." [Page 4]

### 3.4. Existing Models for Smart Manufacturing Adoption

- Cyber-physical systems (CPS): "CPS, as such, can be perceived as a technical umbrella term within industry 4.0 that covers a broad area from the backend software ... to the very frontend interfaces and machines" [Page 4].
- Reference architecture models "are currently not concerned with human factors, despite being crucial to their successful implementation (Sharpe et al. 2019). They merely adopt a technical focus (see Table 1)." [Page 4]
- Table 1 classifies I4.0 models by type, focus, and view on competences. Reference architecture (RA) models listed include RAMI4.0, IIRA, SITAM, IVRA Next, IBM Industry 4.0, LASFA, 5C — all marked **Technical** focus. Process models (PM) listed: SM Adoption Framework (Mittal et al. 2020), Determinants of SM adoption (Ghobakhloo 2020, PM/ISM), Development stages & resources toward I4.0 (Estensoro et al. 2022) — all marked **Socio-technical** focus. [Page 5]
- For SMEs: "a more grounded approach that specifies concrete focus areas may be more useful compared to complete technical reference architectures, which assume a higher digital maturity level than most SMEs possess (Mittal et al. 2018)." [Page 4]

### 3.5. Ghobakhloo's (2020) Model of Determinants

- "Ghobakhloo's model of determinants describes a step-wise process to implement smart technology (see Figure 2)." [Page 5]
- Stepwise logic: "a company that seeks to implement smart technology has to start with management perceiving the benefits of digitalisation, have financial and management support, and work on strategic roadmaps before employee qualification is undertaken and the technical implementation towards smart manufacturing can move forward." [Page 5]
- The model has **11 dependency themes** [Page 7], abbreviated [Page 9]: "Perceived Benefits (PB), Financial Resource Availability (FRA), Management Support (MS), Strategic Roadmapping for Digitalisation (SRD), Employee Qualification (EQ), Digital Maturity (DM), Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR), Seamless Integration Capability (SIC), Openness to Change (OC), Operations Technology Maturity (OTM), Cybersecurity Maturity (CM)."

### 3.6. Methodology

- Design: "We applied a multiple case study approach to gain detailed insights into how manufacturing SMEs approach competence development and how this, in turn, influences their digital transformation." [Page 6]
- Abductive approach: "we approach the phenomenon of digital transformation abductively to expand the existing model of determinants for digitalisation (i.e. Figure 2) through a multiple case study of SMEs." [Page 6]
- Engagement: "Over the course of three years, we engaged multiple groups of SMEs in four engagements lasting three-four months each" [Page 6].
- **Data selection criteria** [Pages 6–7]: enterprises had to be "small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) within manufacturing" (EU definition: "small = < 50 employees and ≤€ 10 million annual balance sheet; medium-sized = < 250 employees and ≤€ 50 million annual balance sheet (European Commission 2003)"); "Manufacturing companies interested in digital transformation" (operationalised as participation in "Innovation Factory North"); and "SMEs from a broad selection of manufacturing industries."
- **Data collection** via cognitive apprenticeship: "The empirical data was collected through a learning process inspired by cognitive apprenticeship (Collins and Kapur 2014), which engaged managers and operational staff from a total of 30 SMEs." Its four dimensions are Content, Methods (scaffolding), Sequencing, and Sociology. [Page 7]
- Activities: "two physical workshops at the university and two on-site company visits at the company" [Page 7]; the 360 Degree Maturity Assessment (360DMA) "provided the structure for the maturity discussions in Workshop 1" [Page 7]. Data collection: Workshop "360 hrs*"; On-site company visits "75 hrs" [Page 8].
- Data extraction: "no voice recordings were made. However, the note-takers were instructed to type up responses as verbatim as possible." [Page 7]
- **Data analysis:** "414 unique statements ... were extracted and processed using QSR NVivo" [Page 7]. Two stages: "i) identification of statements which fit into the model of determinants ... referred to as the engagement cluster (EC); and ii) identification and analysis of the statements which did not fit the model of determinants referred to as the hindrance cluster (HC)." [Page 7]

### 3.7. Engagement Cluster (EC) and Hindrance Cluster (HC)

- "147 statements matched the model of determinants on some level of engagement" [Page 8]; coded as High/Medium/Low.
  - High: "expressions of clear and dedicated activities, strategies or knowledge" [Page 8]
  - Medium: "expressions of activities, strategies or knowledge lacking indication of either high or low levels of engagement" [Page 9]
  - Low: "expressions of sparse and unspecific activities, strategies, or knowledge" [Page 9]
- "267 statements were grouped in the hindrance cluster, as the statements either were a mismatch with the dependency themes, or the statements expressed a specific lack of activities related to the dependency themes." [Page 10] (Composed of "178 statements" relating to the 11 themes but expressing uncertainties/challenges, plus "88 statements which did not match any of the 11 themes." [Page 9])
- HC analysed via the **Gioia methodology**: first-order concepts, second-order themes, aggregate dimensions. "we applied the approach and terminology introduced by Gioia and Chittipeddi (1991) and later updated by Gioia, Corley, and Hamilton (2013)." [Page 9] Example: "14 sample statements were grouped together to form the first-order concept 'manual data processes'." [Page 9]

### 3.8. Findings — Engagement Cluster (per theme)

- **Perceived benefits:** included "cost reduction, automatic data projection and data flow, leading to less employee stress." "The SMEs often use vague terms related to specific technologies with few examples of actual value added." [Page 10]
- **Financial resources / management support / strategic roadmapping:** "there is a tendency for management support within most of the companies" [Page 11]; "Strategic roadmapping for digitalisation is where the biggest uncertainty lies, where many express the lack of a digital strategy." [Page 11]
- **Employee qualifications:** "mentioned, but on a very basic level, e.g. ad hoc peer-to-peer training. ... few prioritise upskilling and training" [Pages 11].
- **Digitalisation maturity:** "this is the theme that the companies tend to struggle the most with, especially aspects surrounding data collection and exploitation. In many instances, data is collected but with no apparent use." [Page 11]
- **CSR / seamless integration / openness to change:** "Overall a very low level of engagement with corporate social responsibility was portrayed." "integration capability is an area where the SMEs struggle most" [Page 11]; "there was a general openness towards new initiatives and technology." [Page 11]
- **Operations technology and cybersecurity maturity:** "The SMEs tend to focus more on operations technology such as robots and 3D printing compared to cybersecurity, which only one statement touched upon" [Page 12]; "SMEs prioritise physical hardware solutions." [Page 12]
- **Sub-conclusion:** productive activity "Especially within management support, openness to change, operations technology maturity and, perhaps surprisingly, financial resource availability." But challenges in "perceived benefits, strategic roadmapping for digitalisation, employees' qualification, digitalisation maturity, corporate social responsibility policy, seamless integration capability and cybersecurity maturity." [Page 11]

### 3.9. Findings — Hindrance Cluster (Aggregate Dimensions)

Two aggregate dimensions emerged:

- **Content knowledge** (first aggregate dimension): "describes the lack of understanding of industry 4.0 and related technologies both from the management and shop-floor level." Three second-order themes: "lack of understanding, data handling, and integration challenges." [Page 12]
  - *Lack of understanding:* "centred around a lack of understanding of digital technologies and the overall concept of industry 4.0." [Page 12]
  - *Data handling:* "the majority do not use the collected data. The data quality is also currently a concern" [Page 12]; "the companies currently rely on many manual processes" [Pages 12–13].
  - *Integration challenges:* "how SMEs struggle to establish effective connectivity between systems to enable such integration." [Page 13]
- **Meta-cognitive knowledge** (second aggregate dimension): "captures the struggle for the SMEs to work on digital transformation strategies ... Meta-cognitive knowledge thus describes insights into how to learn and awareness of the processes involved." Two themes: "upskilling and competence needs and strategic management capabilities." [Page 13]
  - *Upskilling and competence needs:* "the SMEs are strong within classical operational competencies ... but lack experience with digital IT systems." [Page 13]
  - *Strategic management capabilities:* "the vast majority do not have any strategy when it comes to digital technologies. The companies struggle with change management" [Page 13].

### 3.10. Implications for the Model — "Level 0"

- "We identify what can be considered a level 0, focusing on their lack of understanding of industry 4.0 concepts, low data handling and integration capability (content knowledge), and strategic management capabilities together with structured upskilling and competence development (meta-cognitive knowledge)." [Page 13]
- The updated model (Figure 5) is "New model of determinants, where observed hindrances are highlighted." [Page 15]
- "the addition of a 'Level 0' to the model of determinants provides a better starting point and knowledge foundation for companies to engage with the dependency themes" [Page 16].

### 3.11. Discussion — Prior Knowledge and Knowledge Inertia

- Prior knowledge framing (Dochy, de Rijdt, and Dyck 2002): "content knowledge and meta-cognitive knowledge are the two main pillars, where content knowledge relates to domain specific knowledge, where meta-cognitive knowledge is more reflective and deals with the understanding of the learning processes" [Page 16].
- Knowledge inertia (Liao, Fei, and Liu 2008): "Knowledge inertia locks companies into a finite set of processes and knowledge defined by past experience" [Page 16].
- Event horizon: SMEs "have yet to reach the event horizon (i.e. the point of no return), where they leave knowledge inertia behind and become self-sufficient" [Page 17].

### 3.12. Propositions

- **Proposition 1:** "Challenges towards digitalisation experienced by SMEs can be mitigated by an increased focus on both their understanding of content knowledge and meta-cognitive knowledge related to industry 4.0. ..." [Page 16]
- **Proposition 2:** "A lack of foundational prior knowledge leads to knowledge inertia, which explains lack of progress regarding digitalisation observed in manufacturing SMEs." [Page 16]
- **Proposition 3:** "Technology investment alone is not enough to succeed with digitalisation. A supportive scaffold to help with the facilitation of digitalisation initiatives is needed to escape the knowledge inertia present in SMEs and help guide strategies for competence development in parallel with operational technology implementation." [Page 17]

### 3.13. Conclusion and Contributions

- "Our study, based on a sample of 30 SMEs, reveals that important determinants for digitalisation are overlooked, often leading back to an experienced lack of knowledge" [Pages 17–18].
- "Level 0 should target content knowledge ... Additionally, level 0 should target meta-cognitive knowledge related to strategic management of digital technologies" [Page 18].
- "This study makes four key contributions, which materialise in three propositions and an expanded model of determinants for smart technology adoption." [Page 18] The summarised propositions: (1) "SMEs need a knowledge starting point before tackling later stages towards digitalisation: this includes content knowledge (what) and meta-cognitive knowledge (how)." (2) knowledge inertia explains lack of progress. (3) "Technology investments are not enough for digitalisation. Competence development strategies should be developed in parallel with operational technology implementation, preferably through structured facilitation (e.g. a scaffolding approach." [Page 18]

### 3.14. Sample Characteristics (Table 2)

The study sample comprises 30 SMEs (Small: <50 employees; Medium: <250 employees) across industries including Water Cutting, Steel and Metalwork (most frequent), Food, Industrial Freezing, Home and Living, Automation, Ventilation, Entertainment Equipment, Energy, Process Manufacturing, Electronic Hardware, and Hydraulics, with participating representatives ranging from CEOs/COOs/CFOs/CTOs to production, quality, logistics, and engineering managers. [Page 8]

### 3.16. Research Perspectives / Limitations

- "Future research directions should include in-depth case studies of SMEs who have managed to overcome the challenges outlined in this paper" [Page 16].
- Context limitation: "different patterns may emerge in other cultural contexts and economies compared to Denmark." [Page 16]
