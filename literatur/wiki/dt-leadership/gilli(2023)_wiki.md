## 2. Overview

The study aims to provide "empirical evidence regarding competencies sought by practitioners for managing digital transformation" by analyzing "239 job advertisements targeting digital transformation experts and examine the skills and traits explicitly called for" [Page 1].

Stated objective: "the objective of this study is to explore and to categorize leadership competencies for DT which are considered necessary by practitioners" [Page 3], and "we determined the extent to which skills and traits of leaders sought by practitioners to manage DT match competencies identified in conceptual studies" [Page 3].

Headline finding from the abstract: "Our results reveal that technical skills and in-depth expertise in information technologies play only a secondary role in job requirement profiles." Digital transformation experts "should primarily possess collaboration, strategic thinking, leadership, customer orientation, and communication skills." The analyses also "emphasize the importance of specific personality traits, such as proactivity and creativity" [Page 1].

## 3. Core Concepts and Definitions

### 3.1 Digital Transformation (DT)

Defined as "a process that aims to improve an entity by triggering significant changes to its properties through combinations of information, computing, communication, and connectivity technologies" (citing Vial, 2019: 3) [Page 2]. It "is characterized by velocity and a holistic nature" and "does not only affect product and service offerings of organizations, but their organizational processes, systems, and operations, too" [Page 2]. It "requires an organization-wide change program" [Page 2].

### 3.2 The research gap addressed

"What is lacking so far, with rare exceptions, are studies that analyze actual job requirement profiles for leaders of the DT and compare them to these conceptual models" [Page 3]. The authors note that, to their best knowledge, "there is only one study which has analyzed leadership requirements for DT based on job advertisements (Klus and Müller, 2020)" and that since it focuses on "just one specific region in Germany, generalizability of results may be limited" [Page 3].

There is "ample evidence that many DT processes have failed due to the lack of leadership skills rather than the primary lack of knowledge about digital technologies" [Page 2].

### 3.3 Twenty-first century skills

The "concept of 21st century skills attempts to derive important leadership and working skills from trends such as globalization, digitalization and increase of knowledge work" [Page 4]. The Partnership for 21st Century Skills (2008) "proposed three types of 21st century skills: learning skills (creativity and innovation, critical thinking and problem solving, communication and collaboration), literacy skills (information, media and ICT literacy), and life skills (flexibility and adaptability, initiative and self-direction, social and cross-cultural skills, productivity and accountability, leadership and responsibility)" [Page 4].

This concept was deemed not an appropriate framework: "it does not provide an appropriate framework to guide the search for leadership competencies needed for DT" [Page 4].

### 3.4 Leadership competencies = skills + traits

Per Boyatzis (1982), competencies include "traits, self-concept, social role, knowledge, and skills" [Page 4]. For this paper, "skills and traits are therefore considered as the main building blocks of leadership competencies for DT" [Page 5].

- **Skill** (Katz, 1955): "an ability which can be developed, not necessarily inborn, and which is manifested in performance, not merely in potential" [Page 5]. Katz identified three skills for the ideal executive: "technical, conceptual, and human" [Page 5].
- **Traits** (Zaccaro et al., 2004: 104): "relatively stable and coherent integrations of personal characteristics that foster a consistent pattern of leadership performance across a variety of group and organizational situations" [Page 5]. Traits "cannot be learned—at least not in the short-term—and are difficult to change" [Page 5]. Leadership traits are "only a necessary but not sufficient precondition for leadership success" [Page 6].

### 3.5 The Mumford et al. (2007) leadership skills strataplex

This is the chosen "organizing framework" [Page 5]. Skill requirements are "layered (strata) and segmented (plex)" and categorized into "four general categories: (1) cognitive, (2) interpersonal, (3) business, and (4) strategic skills" [Page 5]. The framework emphasizes "that the effectiveness of leadership skills depends on the characteristics and demands of the specific context" [Page 5].

Table 1 lists the framework skills [Page 6]:
- **Cognitive skills:** Communication skills, Listening, Active learning, Critical thinking, Problem identification.
- **Interpersonal skills:** Social perceptiveness, Coordination, Ability to learn and adapt, Establishing relationships, Persuasion skills.
- **Business skills:** Operations analysis, Mgmt. of personnel resources, Mgmt. of financial resources, Mgmt. of material resources.
- **Strategic skills:** Visioning, Systems perception, Systems evaluation, Identification of downstream consequences, Identification of key causes, Solution appraisal.

## 4. Methodology

### 4.1 Approach

An "exploratory research approach" using content analysis of job advertisements [Page 6]. Citing Todd et al. (1995: 24), organizations are "careful to describe skills they actually require" [Page 7]. Job advertisements "need to be as precise as possible so that they mitigate adverse selection problems, are comparatively unbiased, and can be analyzed over time" [Page 7].

### 4.2 Data collection and sampling

- Sources: "Monster, Vault (collaborating with Indeed), and Digital McKinsey from August to September 2018" [Page 7]. "Digital McKinsey has been included as a source" because McKinsey & Company "is particularly active in the field of advising clients in DT" [Page 7].
- Job-title search words: "DT manager, business transformation manager, chief digital officer (CDO), digital strategy consultant, and digital project manager" [Page 7].
- For Monster and Vault, "digital" was the main search term combined with "strategy," "transformation," "consultant," or "project manager" [Page 7]. "The Python software was used to automatically download the content of job advertisements" [Page 7].
- "The initial sample consisted of 492 job advertisements" [Page 7]. After manual review and exclusions (e.g., software developer, web publisher; very narrow digital activities; non-DT-related; trainee positions), "we were left with a final sample of 239 advertisements" [Page 7–8].

**Sample composition (of 239):**
- Location: "24 were from firms located in Europe, 213 in North America, and 2 in Australia" [Page 8].
- Experience: "96 advertisements related to senior positions (explicitly calling for more than 5 years of professional experience) and 44 to junior positions"; "The remaining 99 job advertisements asked for experience without any further temporal specification" [Page 8].
- Employers: "only private-sector companies of various sizes from a range of sectors, with consulting firms representing by far the largest share (62%)" [Page 8].

### 4.3 Data analysis

- Content analysis coding skills and traits, using "the MAX QDA software" and applying "the Gioia method (Gioia et al., 2013)" [Page 8].
- First-order analysis: "Out of 2781 coded segments, we generated 144 first-order concepts" [Page 8].
- Second-order analysis: "After checking for category nesting and overlap, 47 second-order themes emerged" [Page 8].
- Four dimensions used to assign themes: "qualification, experience, skills, and traits" [Page 8] (following Klus and Müller, 2020).
- "knowledge" was eliminated "because knowledge overlaps with other job requirements" [Page 8].
- "To reduce complexity and eliminate outliers we decided to remove specific job requirements mentioned less than 10 times leaving us with a final total of 29 second-order themes" [Page 8].
- Taxonomy: an "abductive approach, as suggested by Kalargyrou et al. (2012)" assigning each skill to one of the four Mumford et al. (2007) categories [Page 10].

## 5. Results / Findings

### 5.1 Top five leadership competencies for DT (Table 4)

"the five most frequently requested competencies for managing DT within organizations based on our second-order clustering are: collaboration, strategic thinking, team leadership, customer orientation, and communication skills" [Page 13].

| No. | Leadership competency | Frequency |
|-----|----------------------|-----------|
| 1 | Collaboration | (199) |
| 2 | Strategic thinking | (185) |
| 3 | Team leadership skills | (165) |
| 4 | Customer orientation | (152) |
| 5 | Communication skills | (137) |

(Source: Table 4 [Page 13].)

### 5.2 Distribution across the four Mumford categories

"all skill categories of the Mumford et al. (2007) framework—cognitive skills (n = 432), interpersonal skills (n = 465), business skills (n = 598), and strategic skills (n = 499)—show a fairly even distribution" [Page 13].

### 5.3 Skills detail (Table 2)

Second-order themes with frequencies, by Mumford category [Pages 11–12]:
- **Cognitive skills:** Communication skills (137); Problem solving (85); Analytical skills (52); Presentation skills (48); Multi-tasking (39); Time management (37); Adaptability (13); Explain complex issues in a simple manner (11); Listening (10).
- **Interpersonal skills:** Collaboration (199); Team leadership (165); Relationship management (101).
- **Business skills:** Project management (121); Big Data analysis (117); Technological expertise (103); Specific technological skills (88); Coding & Programing (61); Agile & Scrum (56); Business-savvyness (27); E-Commerce (14); Design thinking (11).
- **Strategic skills:** Strategic thinking (185); Customer orientation (152); Change management (95); Business development (45); Innovation attitude (22).

### 5.4 Traits detail (Table 3)

The four trait second-order themes "proposed are pro-activeness, willingness to travel, creativity, and passion" [Page 10]; frequencies [Page 12]:
- Pro-activeness (54) — first-order: Driven, Self-starter, Pro-active, Experimental.
- Willingness to travel (40).
- Creativity (18) — first-order: Creative, Think outside the box.
- Passion (14) — first-order: Passion, Interest, Enthusiasm, Motivation.

### 5.5 Notable descriptive observations

- More skill themes than trait themes: "26 second-order skill themes compared to four second-order trait themes" [Page 10].
- "we believe it is surprising that personality traits are mentioned at all in job advertisements relating to DT" [Page 10].
- Technical skills are secondary, yet digitalization technologies "are reflected by the high number of mentions (more than 280) in job postings regarding DT" [Page 10].
- "key words and terms which are commonly closely related to DT such as Industry 4.0, Internet of things, artificial intelligence, blockchain, or augmented reality did not show up at all" [Page 10].
- Collaboration: "explicitly mentioned in more than 83% of all analyzed job postings (n = 199)" [Page 14].
- Communication: "explicitly mentioned as a necessary skill set in more than 57% of all job postings (n = 137)"; if grouped with presentation/listening/explaining, "more than four-fifths of all job advertisements contain these skills (n = 206)" [Page 14].
- "relatively few job descriptions include creativity (n = 18; 7.5%) or passion (n = 14; 5.9%)" [Page 14].

### 5.6 Qualifications and prior experience

- An academic degree "is mentioned as a requirement in almost all cases analyzed (84.5%)," but "a certain level of higher education is often not mandated" and "no specific discipline dominates DT-related job postings" [Page 14].
- Consulting experience: "Two-thirds of all job postings by consulting firms mentioned the need or preference for applicants to have prior consulting experience (97 out of 148)," similarly "industry—or subject-specific experience among the other industries (60 out of 91)" [Page 15].

## 6. Comparison with Previous Studies

### 6.1 Versus information/computer-science conceptual studies

Such studies "particularly emphasize knowledge in different technology fields"; the "Skills for Digital Transformation" study (Hoberg et al., 2015) is "a prototypical example," with response categories limited to predefined importance–availability pairs (e.g., "Business Change Management (84%–46%)," "Internet of Things (75%–32%)," "Digital Security (88%–45%)") [Page 15]. In that framing, important strategic/interpersonal skills "are absent or wrongly assumed to be either not critical for DT or anyhow present in organizations" [Page 16].

### 6.2 Versus practice-based CDO/CIO/CTO conceptualizations

These "frequently use aggregated and comparatively vague terminology, which does not appear suitable for establishing a clear requirement profile" [Page 16].

### 6.3 Versus literature reviews

"beyond generic success attributes of leaders (e.g., social intelligence, rewarding, agile, visionary), clearly defining skills and personality traits is necessary to allow operationalization" [Page 16]. The results "provide empirical evidence that general managerial and leadership skills are at least equally relevant as skills related to specific (digital) technologies" [Page 16], supporting "the assumption that change processes that constitute DT are not that different from other organizational change interventions" [Page 16].

### 6.4 Versus 21st century skills

There are "certain overlaps in the requirements, for example, regarding collaboration and communication skills," but "significant gaps, for example, with regard to team leadership, project management or strategic skills are obvious" [Pages 16–17].

### 6.5 Versus Klus and Müller (2020)

Klus and Müller "applied a similar methodological approach" [Page 17]. Key differences: the "high prevalence of interpersonal skills... and strategic skills... which we found in our sample is not reflected in their study" [Page 17]; "pro-activity, which is frequently addressed in our study, does not appear" in theirs [Page 17]; their German sample emphasizes "intercultural competence and the leader's self-motivation," whereas the international sample of this study highlights "the ability to lead cross-functional teams" [Page 17].

## 7. Discussion and Conclusions

- General finding: "a great variety of leadership skills and traits are expected to ensure successful DT in addition to technical skills and specific knowledge" [Page 18]. The emphasis on communication, cross-functional collaboration, strategic thinking, and customer orientation "bears a strong resemblance to other strategic change processes" [Page 18].
- "people, not technology, drive DT" and organizations "require leaders and not necessarily technological specialists" [Page 18].

### 7.1 Theoretical contributions

- Complements "the comparatively underdeveloped empirical coverage of actual job requirements" [Page 18].
- Applies and transfers "the leadership skills strataplex proposed by Mumford et al. (2007) to a contemporary and novel context" [Page 18].

### 7.2 Practical implications

- Offers "a systematic overview of skills and traits" usable to "check and complement job descriptions and advertisements currently used" [Page 18].
- Helps "mitigate assumptions that successful DT processes are only possible through hiring technological experts" [Page 18].

### 7.3 Limitations

- Choice of organizing framework "will impact the results and their interpretation" [Page 19].
- Sample/database selection: excluding other recruiting websites and firms "may impact the generalizability of our results" [Page 19].
- Some jobs "might not be advertised externally but are filled internally" [Page 19].
- Coding/categorization "needs subjective interpretation," with a "carbage in – carbage out" problem when using content analysis software [Pages 19–20]. (Note: "carbage" appears thus in source.)

### 7.4 Future research

- Compare DT competencies between "large companies" and "small and medium-sized enterprises" [Page 19].
- Compare "private versus those of public administration organizations" [Page 19].
- Cross-national/cross-cultural comparisons; "future research may employ triangulation," including qualitative interviews and observation of DT-process stakeholders [Pages 19–20].
