# Design Science in Information Systems Research

**Authors:** Alan R. Hevner, Salvatore T. March, Jinsoo Park, Sudha Ram
**Venue:** MIS Quarterly, Vol. 28, No. 1, pp. 75–105 (March 2004)
**Method:** Conceptual framework + guidelines + exemplar analysis

---

## Research Objective

Describe the performance of design-science research in IS via a concise conceptual framework and clear guidelines for understanding, executing, and evaluating the research.

## Core Argument: Two Complementary Paradigms

IS research involves two complementary paradigms:
- **Behavioral science** — rooted in natural science; develops and justifies *theories* that explain or predict organizational/human phenomena
- **Design science** — rooted in engineering/sciences of the artificial (Simon 1996); creates and evaluates *artifacts* intended to solve organizational problems

Truth (behavioral) and utility (design) are inseparable — each informs the other.

## IS Research Framework (Figure 2)

Three interconnected components:

| Component | Contains | Role |
|-----------|----------|------|
| **Environment** | People (roles, capabilities), Organizations (strategies, structure, culture), Technology (infrastructure, apps) | Defines the problem space; provides **relevance** |
| **IS Research** | Develop/Build (theories, artifacts) ↔ Justify/Evaluate (analytical, case study, experimental, field study, simulation) | The research activities; iterative build-evaluate loop |
| **Knowledge Base** | Foundations (theories, frameworks, instruments, constructs, models, methods, instantiations) + Methodologies (data analysis, formalisms, measures, validation criteria) | Provides **rigor**; raw materials for research |

Business needs flow from Environment → IS Research. Applicable knowledge flows from IS Research → Knowledge Base. The assess/refine cycle iterates between build and evaluate.

## Four Types of IT Artifacts

Following March & Smith (1995):

| Artifact Type | Definition |
|--------------|-----------|
| **Constructs** | Vocabulary and symbols — the language for defining problems and solutions |
| **Models** | Abstractions and representations — connect problem and solution components |
| **Methods** | Algorithms and practices — guidance on how to search the solution space |
| **Instantiations** | Implemented and prototype systems — demonstrate feasibility |

## The 7 Guidelines (Table 1)

| # | Guideline | Description |
|---|-----------|-------------|
| 1 | **Design as an Artifact** | Must produce a viable artifact: construct, model, method, or instantiation |
| 2 | **Problem Relevance** | Develop technology-based solutions to important and relevant business problems |
| 3 | **Design Evaluation** | Utility, quality, and efficacy must be rigorously demonstrated via well-executed evaluation methods |
| 4 | **Research Contributions** | Must provide clear and verifiable contributions in artifact, foundations, and/or methodologies |
| 5 | **Research Rigor** | Rigorous methods in both construction and evaluation of the artifact |
| 6 | **Design as a Search Process** | Utilize available means to reach desired ends while satisfying laws in the problem environment |
| 7 | **Communication of Research** | Must be presented effectively to both technology-oriented and management-oriented audiences |

## Design Evaluation Methods (Table 2)

| Category | Methods |
|----------|---------|
| **Observational** | Case study, field study |
| **Analytical** | Static analysis, architecture analysis, optimization, dynamic analysis |
| **Experimental** | Controlled experiment, simulation |
| **Testing** | Functional (black box), structural (white box) |
| **Descriptive** | Informed argument, scenarios |

## Key Distinctions

- **Routine design vs. design-science research:** Routine design applies existing knowledge to known problems. DSR addresses *important unsolved problems* in unique or innovative ways. The differentiator is a clear contribution to the knowledge base.
- **Wicked problems:** DSR addresses problems characterized by: unstable requirements, complex interactions, inherent flexibility to change, critical dependence on human cognitive and social abilities.
- **Design as a search process:** Design is essentially a search for an effective solution — the Generate/Test Cycle (Figure 3). Satisficing (Simon 1996) is appropriate when optimal solutions are intractable.

## Three Exemplar Analyses

The paper analyzes three published papers against the 7 guidelines:
1. **Gavish & Gerdes (1998)** — GDSS anonymity mechanisms: strong on artifact, rigor, and evaluation (formal proofs + cost-benefit); weak on communication to managerial audience
2. **Aalst & Kumar (2003)** — XRL workflow language: strong artifact (Petri-net formal semantics), good search process; no instantiation evaluation
3. **Markus, Majchrzak & Gasser (2002)** — Design theory for emergent knowledge processes: strongest on contribution and communication; design theory rather than instantiation
