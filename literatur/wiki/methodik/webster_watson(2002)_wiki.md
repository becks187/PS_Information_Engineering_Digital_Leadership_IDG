# Analyzing the Past to Prepare for the Future: Writing a Literature Review

**Authors:** Jane Webster, Richard T. Watson
**Venue:** MIS Quarterly, Vol. 26, No. 2, pp. xiii–xxiii (June 2002)
**Type:** Guest editorial / methodological guide

---

## Research Objective

Provide guidelines for writing high-quality IS literature review articles, addressing the gap in structured review methodology within the IS discipline.

## Why Literature Reviews Matter

- Few published review articles exist in IS — impeding theoretical progress
- IS has developed from ad hoc classification systems → taxonomies → conceptual frameworks → approaching theoretical systems (Parsons & Shils 1962)
- MISQ Review was created specifically to accelerate IS knowledge accumulation through review articles
- Reviews facilitate theory development, close areas where research exists, and uncover areas where research is needed

## Structured Search Strategy

A systematic 3-step approach to identify source material:

1. **Start with leading journals** — major contributions are found there first. Use databases (ABI/Inform, ProQuest), scan tables of contents. Also examine conference proceedings with strong reputations. Must not limit to North American or a small set of "top" journals
2. **Go backward** — review the citations of articles identified in step 1 to find prior foundational work
3. **Go forward** — use citation indices (Web of Science / SSCI) to identify articles *citing* the key articles from steps 1 and 2

Completion signal: when no new concepts are appearing in the article set.

## Concept-Centric vs. Author-Centric Reviews (Table 1)

| Approach | Structure | Quality |
|----------|-----------|---------|
| **Concept-centric** | "Concept X ... [author A, author B, ...]" | Synthesizes literature; organizes by ideas |
| **Author-centric** | "Author A ... concept X, concept Y, ..." | Produces "phone book" of summaries; fails to synthesize |

**Key principle:** A literature review must be **concept-centric** — concepts, not authors, determine the organizing framework.

## The Concept Matrix (Table 2)

A tool for transitioning from author-centric reading to concept-centric synthesis:

| Articles | Concept A | Concept B | Concept C | Concept D | ... |
|----------|-----------|-----------|-----------|-----------|-----|
| Article 1 | | x | | | x |
| Article 2 | x | x | | | |
| ... | | | x | x | |

- Compile the matrix as you read each article
- When reading is complete, synthesize by discussing each identified concept
- Develop a logical approach to grouping and presenting key concepts

### Extended Concept Matrix with Units of Analysis (Table 3)

Add a dimension for unit of analysis (O = organizational, G = group, I = individual) to isolate concepts by scope — produces crisper review, easier to detect when a concept strays outside its domain.

## Writing the Review Article

### Beginning
- Hook the reader early — motivate your topic, provide working definitions, clearly articulate contributions
- Ways to demonstrate contribution: new theoretical understanding, noting little prior research, calls from respected academics, bringing together disparate streams, important practical implications

### Setting Boundaries
- Define level(s) of analysis, temporal and contextual limitations, scope, values bounding the theory
- State what literature/fields will be drawn upon and why

### Tables and Figures
- Effective for communicating major findings, but must categorize articles by a meaningful scheme — not merely list them
- Tables should define the topic area (types of variables, level of analysis, gaps)

### Tone
- Constructive, not overly critical — "explain how research builds upon previous findings rather than claim previous research is inadequate"
- Be fault tolerant; knowledge accumulates slowly and piecemeal

### Tense
- Prefer present tense (concepts are always here and now)
- Use past tense only when attributing a statement to a specific person ("Weber once said...")

## Theoretical Development

- A review must identify critical knowledge gaps and chart a path for future research
- Develop a conceptual model with supporting propositions
- Propositions are justified from three sources: theoretical explanations ("why"), past empirical findings, and practice/experience
- The "why" (logical reasoning) is the most important — it is "the theoretical glue that welds the model together" (Whetten 1989)
- Models can be variance (factor) models or process models; best reviews draw from both

## Evaluating Your Theory

Good theories should: explain, predict, and delight (Weick 1995); be parsimonious, falsifiable, and useful (Sutton & Staw 1995); exhibit creativity, relevance, and comprehensiveness.

## Summary: The Ideal Review Article

1. Motivates the research topic and explains contributions
2. Describes key concepts
3. Delineates boundaries of the research
4. Reviews relevant prior literature in IS and related areas
5. Develops a model to guide future research
6. Justifies propositions with theoretical explanations, empirical findings, and practical examples
7. Presents concluding implications for researchers and managers

Reviewers look for: **contribution** ("what's new?"), **impact** ("so what?"), **logic** ("why so?"), and **thoroughness** ("well done?") (Whetten 1989).

## Relevance to the Proseminar Paper

- **Concept-centric structure:** The proseminar's literature review must organize DT leadership competencies by concept (e.g., self-awareness, EI, systems thinking), not by author — the concept matrix is the tool for this
- **Concept matrix:** Can be used to show which DT leadership sources address which IDG dimensions — this becomes the analytical backbone of the artifact
- **Search strategy:** The go-backward/go-forward approach justifies the proseminar's literature selection process
- **Tone guidance:** Build on prior DT leadership frameworks rather than criticizing their lack of inner skills — frame IDG as a *complement*, not a correction
- **Theoretical development:** The proseminar's contribution is a conceptual model (IDG mapped onto DT leadership) with propositions about why inner skills matter for DT leaders
