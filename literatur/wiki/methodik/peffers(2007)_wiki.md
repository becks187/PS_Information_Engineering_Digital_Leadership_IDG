# A Design Science Research Methodology for Information Systems Research

**Authors:** Ken Peffers, Tuure Tuunanen, Marcus A. Rothenberger, Samir Chatterjee
**Venue:** Journal of Management Information Systems, Vol. 24, No. 3, pp. 45–77 (Winter 2007–08)
**DOI:** 10.2753/MIS0742-1222240302
**Method:** Consensus-building from 7 prior DS frameworks + 4 case study demonstrations

---

## Research Objective

Develop a commonly accepted design science research methodology (DSRM) for IS that provides: (1) a nominal process for conducting DS research, (2) builds on prior literature, and (3) provides a mental model/template for research outputs.

## The Problem

- DS research has been slow to diffuse in IS despite 15+ years of advocacy
- Lack of a commonly accepted process model may explain why
- Principles ([[source-hevner-2004-dsr-guidelines]]) and practice rules exist, but no generally accepted *procedure* for carrying out and presenting DS research
- A methodology requires three elements: conceptual principles, practice rules, and a **process**

## The DSRM Process Model (Figure 1)

Six activities in a nominal sequence, with process iteration:

### Activity 1: Problem Identification and Motivation
- Define the specific research problem and justify the value of a solution
- Atomize the problem conceptually so the solution can capture its complexity
- Motivates the researcher and audience to pursue and accept the results

### Activity 2: Define the Objectives for a Solution
- Infer objectives from the problem definition and knowledge of what is possible/feasible
- Can be quantitative (better than current) or qualitative (description of how a new artifact supports previously unaddressed problems)
- Should be inferred rationally from the problem specification

### Activity 3: Design and Development
- Create the artifact: constructs, models, methods, or instantiations
- Determine desired functionality and architecture, then create the actual artifact
- Draw on knowledge of theory that can be brought to bear in a solution

### Activity 4: Demonstration
- Demonstrate the use of the artifact to solve one or more instances of the problem
- Could involve experimentation, simulation, case study, proof, or other activity

### Activity 5: Evaluation
- Observe and measure how well the artifact supports a solution to the problem
- Compare objectives from Activity 2 to actual observed results from Activity 4
- Can iterate back to Activity 3 (design) to improve, or proceed to communication

### Activity 6: Communication
- Communicate the problem and its importance, the artifact, its utility and novelty, the rigor of its design, and its effectiveness
- Structure can mirror empirical research: problem → lit review → method → results → discussion → conclusion

## Four Entry Points (Figure 1, bottom)

The process is nominally sequential but researchers can enter at different points:

| Entry Point | Starting Activity | Trigger |
|-------------|------------------|---------|
| **Problem-Centered** | Activity 1 | Observation of a problem or suggested future research from a prior paper |
| **Objective-Centered** | Activity 2 | Industry/research need that can be addressed by developing an artifact |
| **Design & Development-Centered** | Activity 3 | An existing artifact from another domain that hasn't been formally applied to a new problem |
| **Client/Context-Initiated** | Activity 4 | Observing a practical solution that worked; researchers apply rigor retroactively |

## Synthesis from 7 Prior Frameworks (Table 1)

The DSRM synthesizes process elements from:
- Archer (1954): programming, data collection, analysis, synthesis, development
- Takeda et al. (1990): problem enumeration, suggestion, development, confirmatory evaluation
- Eekels & Roozenburg (1991): analysis, requirements, synthesis, simulation, evaluation, decision
- Nunamaker et al. (1991): conceptual framework → system architecture → build → experiment/observe/evaluate
- Walls et al. (1992): meta-requirements, kernel theories → design method, meta design → testable hypotheses
- Cole et al. (2005) / Rossi & Sein (2003): identify need → build → evaluate
- Hevner et al. (2004): important problems → iterative search process → artifact → evaluate → communication

All 7 agree on: problem identification, design & development, and evaluation as core elements. Communication is explicitly noted by Archer and Hevner.

## Four Case Study Demonstrations

Each case is analyzed through the DSRM lens:

1. **CATCH Data Warehouse** (Berndt & Hevner) — problem-centered; community health assessment automation; evaluated by 10+ years of real use in 20+ U.S. counties
2. **Software Reuse Measure** (Rothenberger & Hershauer) — objective-centered; generic reuse metric applied to enterprise model-based development; evaluated via 5 projects (57–143 KLOC)
3. **SIP-Based VoIP Application** (Chatterjee et al.) — design & development-centered; deployed across 202 universities via Internet2
4. **Critical Success Chains** (Peffers & Tuunanen) — problem-centered; method for generating new ideas for mobile financial services; evaluated via survey of industry experts

## Relevance to the Proseminar Paper

- **Process template:** The DSRM 6-step model is the methodological backbone for the proseminar paper — cite this as the DSR approach adopted
- **Entry point:** The proseminar is best described as **design & development-centered** (Activity 3 entry) — the IDG framework exists in sustainability leadership and is being extended to DT leadership. Alternatively, it could be framed as **problem-centered** (Activity 1) starting from the gap identified in the literature
- **Scope calibration:** For a 3 ECTS proseminar, Activities 1–3 (problem → objectives → design) are the core deliverables. Activity 4 (demonstration) can be a scenario/example. Activity 5 (evaluation) can be descriptive/informed argument. Activity 6 (communication) is the paper itself
- **Legitimacy:** Citing DSRM gives the proseminar methodological credibility — it follows an established, widely-cited process rather than an ad hoc approach
